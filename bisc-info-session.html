Read this first

Your 45 minutes is not a 45-minute talk. It's the same short conversation, six or seven times over, with different people.

What the 45 minutes actually looks like

2:35–2:45 · The rush. Everyone arrives at once. You may have four people standing around you. Don't try to talk to each separately — run one conversation out loud and let the others listen in.

2:45–3:10 · The middle. The real work. People arrive in ones and twos, stay eight or ten minutes, move on.

3:10–3:20 · The tail. It thins. Finish capture, catch anyone hovering, get commitments from whoever's still there.

The two of you

One talks. One writes. Agree which before 2:35, and swap halfway if you want.

The one talking never breaks eye contact to write something down. That's the whole reason there are two of you.

The one writing catches names and contact details, and watches the edge of the group for the person hovering who hasn't spoken. Go get them — that person is usually the one who most needed to be there.

Five rules for the table

  • Ask before you tell. Every conversation opens with a question.
  • Never ask anyone for their story. Not here, not as a warm-up. If they offer it, listen. Don't request it.
  • One ask per person. Don't stack three requests. Get the one that matters and stop.
  • Say "I don't know" plainly. Write the question down, say who'll follow up. Guessing costs more than not knowing.
  • End conversations on purpose. "I want to catch a few more people — can I get your email and follow up properly?" is a complete, friendly exit.

When nobody's at your table

It will happen, probably around 3:05. Don't stand there looking at your phone.

  • Write up the conversations you just had, while you remember them
  • Find someone standing alone and go say hello
  • Check whether another table needs a hand

An empty table isn't a failure. Three real conversations beat twelve rushed ones.

What every table captures

Same four things at all three tables, on paper, as it happens:

  • Name
  • Email (and phone if offered)
  • Who they are — pilot, organization, or funder
  • The one thing to do next, and by when

Hand every sheet to the Host before you leave the room.

Table 1 · Prospective members

People who were in a pilot and haven't joined. Get them signed up before they walk away.

Remember all afternoon: most people at this table have never met another pilot participant. That fact does more work than anything you can say. Let it happen before you start explaining.

The conversation · about 8 minutes

1

1 minute

Open with a question

"Which pilot were you in?"Then stop talking. Let them answer properly.

Follow with "How long ago did it end?" or "What was that like?" — and mean it. You're not waiting for your turn.

2

30 seconds

Connect them to the room

If someone else at the table was in a pilot, introduce them. If you were in one, say which.

"You're the third person at this table today from a Texas pilot."

This is the moment the union stops being an abstraction.

3

90 seconds

What BISC is

"About eighty of us met at this conference last year, in a closed session for pilot participants. We realized we'd all been studied, written about, and quoted — and none of us had ever met each other. So we built a union. People who've been through pilots, organizing for a basic income for everyone."

Don't go longer unless they ask. The next beat matters more.

4

2 minutes

What membership actually involves

  • It's free. No dues, no fees, ever.
  • You don't have to prove anything. You tell us your pilot and we take your word for it.
  • You're never asked to tell your story as the price of belonging. When storytelling is the work, it's chosen and prepared.
  • Nothing is required. A monthly meeting if you want it. Work you pick. Step back any time and you're still a member.
5

1 minute

What happens after they join

"You'll get a welcome email straight away. Within a week, a member — a BISC Buddy — reaches out however you asked, call or text or email. Then there's a new member meeting on the second Wednesday, two sessions so you can pick the time that works."

2 minutes

Sign them up, right now

Not tonight. Not when they get home. Here, phone already in hand.

"Can I get you signed up before you go? It's about two minutes — scan this."Hand them the QR card. Stay with them while they do it.

If they won't do it on their phone, take name and email on paper and sign them up yourself that evening.

7

30 seconds

Close

"Your Buddy will be in touch within the week. If you don't hear from anyone, email me — here's a card."

If they stay longer

  • The app. Show them. The learning tracks land well — twenty-seven modules on speaking, advocacy, and how power works, free, on their phone.
  • The working groups. Storytelling, advocacy, tools and policy, training. Ask which sounds like them and write it down.
  • Becoming a Buddy. For someone clearly ready to do more.
  • Their own cohort. "Are you still in touch with anyone from your pilot?" This is how the union grows — one person bringing twenty.

Questions you'll get

Does this cost anything?

No. No dues, no fees, no minimum. Say it plainly and without hedging.

Will this affect my benefits?
Check the union's current answer before Friday. This was still being confirmed. Don't improvise a benefits answer.

Safe version: "Membership is free and no money changes hands, so there's nothing to report. If you want a proper answer for your own situation, give me your email and we'll get you one from someone who actually knows."

Do I have to prove I was in a pilot?

"No. You tell us your pilot and we take your word for it. You've been asked to prove enough."

I don't want to speak publicly.

"Then don't. Plenty of members never speak publicly. There's tool-building, policy tracking, welcoming new members, running meetings. And you can just be a member."

I'm not really political.

"Neither were most of us. This isn't a party thing. It's people who got money and know what it did, saying so."

My pilot ended years ago.

"That's fine — most of ours did. Current or past, it counts."

What do you do with my information?

"It stays with the union. We don't share or sell it. We use your email to reach you and that's it."

I don't have time for this.

"Nothing's required. Join, see what comes through, do something when you feel like it. Being on the list costs you nothing."

Aim for 6 to 8 sign-ups in the room. Ten is a very good afternoon.

Table 2 · Pilot administrators

The highest-leverage table in the room. One administrator can reach a whole cohort — nothing else here scales like that.

What you're asking for: an introduction to their alumni. Not a contact list, not a data handover. Say that early and unprompted — it removes the fear before they have to voice it.

The conversation · about 12 minutes

1

2 minutes

Ask about their pilot, properly

"Which pilot do you run?" · "How many participants?" · "Where's it at now — still running, or wrapped?"

Be genuinely interested. These are people who built something hard without enough money, and almost nobody asks them about it.

Listen for the one thing that matters: is it ending, or has it ended? That's the opening.

2

2 minutes

Name the problem you both have

"What happens to your participants when the pilot ends?"Let them answer. Most will say some version of: not much, and it bothers them.
"That's the thing we kept running into. Pilots end, the report comes out, everyone scatters. All that experience just disperses."
3

2 minutes

What BISC is, in their terms

"We're a national union of people who've been through pilots. Founded by participants, run by participants. About eighty of us met here at BIG last year and built it from there."
  • Their alumni get somewhere to go when the pilot ends
  • Their participants become experienced advocates for this work
  • They get a channel to people they can no longer contact directly
  • The evidence base gets a constituency, not just a report
4

2 minutes

The ask

"Would you be willing to tell your alumni we exist? Not give us anything — just forward something, or mention us where they already are."

Then, before they ask:

"We're not asking for contact details. You make the introduction, people come to us if they want to. Nothing about your consent commitments changes."
5

3 minutes

Work through whatever comes up

Take their concerns seriously. Don't push. An administrator who feels pressured will politely agree and then do nothing.

If the answer's no, ask what would make it possible. That answer is worth as much as a yes.

6

1 minute

Get a month, and write it down

"Could we set up a proper call? What month works — October, November?"

Write down: name, organization, pilot, month, and anything they said they'd need. A vague "stay in touch" is a no with better manners.

If they stay longer

  • Co-design. Members with lived expertise, prepared and compensated, helping design the next pilot. Often what they actually want.
  • What we hear across pilots. Patterns they can't see from inside one program — payment timing, benefits cliffs, recertification burden.
  • Their current participants. People still enrolled can join too.
  • Speakers. They may want participant voices at their own events. Say yes — and say members are compensated for that work.

Questions you'll get

We made privacy commitments at enrollment. I can't share contacts.

"Completely understood, and we're not asking for that. You send them something, they decide. Nothing comes to us unless a person chooses it."

This is the most common concern. Getting it right is most of the conversation.

Does this cost my organization anything?

"Nothing. No fee, no partnership agreement. We're asking you to forward something."

Are you going to criticize our program?

"Members say what they experienced, and sometimes that's critical of a design choice. What we don't do is attack individual programs or the people running them. Mostly it's aimed at the systems around the pilot — benefits cliffs, payment timing, the proving."

"Frankly, that feedback is more useful to you than a satisfaction survey."

My participants are still enrolled. Is that a problem?

"Not at all. Current or former, both count."

We already have an alumni network.

"That's great, and it's not a competitor. Yours is local — ours is national. Your people can be in both, and a strong local group is exactly who we most want to hear from."

What's your relationship with Income Movement?

"They're a partner. The union was founded by participants and is run by participants — Income Movement supports the work, they don't direct it."

Who's funding you?
Agree the answer with Loren before Friday. Don't name funders who haven't confirmed.

Aim for 3 to 5 real conversations with a named follow-up month. Two good ones beat eight polite ones.

Table 3 · Funders

Be concrete and unembarrassed. This audience respects specificity and distrusts vagueness.

You're not asking for money in this room. You're asking what they'd need to see. Smaller question, easier to answer, and it gets you something real instead of something polite.

Before Friday, get these from Loren: the actual budget ask, fiscal sponsorship status, what's currently funded and by whom, and what's confirmed versus pipeline. Don't work this table without real numbers.

The conversation · about 12 minutes

1

2 minutes

Find out what they fund

"What brings you to BIG?" · "What are you funding in this space at the moment?"

You're listening for whether they fund pilots, advocacy, movement infrastructure, or research. It changes everything you say next.

2

2 minutes

Name the gap

"There's been a lot of money into pilots, and it's produced good evidence. What hasn't been funded is the participants themselves being organized. Forty thousand people have been through a guaranteed income pilot in this country, and until last year almost none of them had met each other."
"That's the most credible constituency this policy area has, and it was completely unorganized."
3

3 minutes

What exists now — be specific

  • An Organizing Committee made entirely of pilot participants, meeting weekly since September
  • A governance structure — membership tiers, voting rights, a conflict process
  • An onboarding system with trained member volunteers
  • A member platform: joining, meetings, agendas, notes
  • A six-track curriculum — twenty-seven modules on advocacy, policy, facilitation, public speaking
  • Soft launch underway, national from there

Show them the app. It's the single most convincing thing you have. It exists, it works, and participants built it.

4

2 minutes

What's next, and what it costs

Use the real figures from Loren.

What the next twelve months look like, what it costs, what's funded, what the gap is. Name a number. Vagueness reads as not having thought it through.

5

1 minute

Member compensation — say it plainly

"One thing we're firm about: members get paid for their work. This is a movement that has asked people to tell their hardest stories for free for years. We're not doing that. It's a principle, not a line item we'll cut when the budget gets tight."

Some funders find this refreshing. Some push back. Either way you've learned something useful about them.

6

2 minutes

The ask

"What would you need to see to consider supporting something like this?"

Write down exactly what they say. That sentence is the whole reason this table exists — it's the follow-up plan, in their words.

Get a card. Give a card.

Questions you'll get

What's your theory of change?

"Forty thousand people have received guaranteed income in this country. Organized, they're the most credible constituency in this policy area — and the only one that can't be dismissed as speculation."

If they want more: pilots build evidence, evidence needs a constituency, and a constituency is what moves federal policy.

Why fund this rather than more pilots?

"Both. But we have a lot of pilot evidence now and not much political power. The 2021 Child Tax Credit cut child poverty to a record low and then expired, because there wasn't an organized constituency to defend it. That's the gap."

What's your legal structure? Are you a 501(c)(3)?
Get the current answer from Loren. Fiscal sponsorship arrangements must be described accurately.
How do you measure success?

Members recruited and retained. Members trained and active. Policy engagements — comments, meetings, testimony. Over time, participants at the table when programs are designed.

Honest addition: "We're building the measurement alongside the organization. I'd rather tell you that than give you a dashboard we invented for this conversation."

Isn't this lobbying?
Confirm with Loren. This has real legal implications and must be answered precisely, not approximately.
Who else is funding you?
Only name confirmed funders. Naming a prospect as a supporter is the fastest way to lose both.
What happens if this doesn't work?

"Then forty thousand people met each other and learned to advocate, and that doesn't un-happen. The curriculum, the connections, the skills — those stay with members whatever becomes of the organization."

Aim for 2 to 4 real conversations with a card and a named next step. One serious funder conversation justifies this table.

Before the conference

1. Program language. Get the two BISC sessions described differently in the program and on door signs, or you'll split your own audience.

2. The look-in. If you want to bring administrators or funders past the Design Sprint, ask the sprint participants first. Frame it as observing the work, not the people. Our own curriculum tells members they are not the exhibit — don't contradict that in front of the people we taught it to.

3. The yellow boxes. Every yellow box in the table guides is something to settle before you travel — the benefits answer, budget figures, legal structure, and which funders can be named.

Afterward

Ten minutes with the seven of you, that evening or the next morning, while you still remember:

  • What landed
  • What didn't
  • Who to follow up with, by name
  • What we'd change

Write it down. It's the only reason the next one is better.
