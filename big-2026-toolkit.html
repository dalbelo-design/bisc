Participant Power Building

90 minutes, main stage. A panel of pilot administrators, facilitated by participants — which is the point.

Why this session matters

In almost every room like this, the pilot administrator leads and the participant answers. Here it is the other way round.

Zyera and Daniela facilitate. The administrators answer. That inversion is not a staging choice — it is the argument the union is making, performed in front of the movement.

The question we are trying to answer

How will building participant power launch our mission of basic income for all?

Who is on stage

  • GroFund — In Her Hands. Participants engaged in design and research.
  • Bread for the City. Participants determine their own payment amounts and schedule.
  • Path Home. Space for participants to connect and build social capital with each other.
  • MyPath — Bill of RYTS. A participant-created set of policy recommendations centring young BIPOC community members.
  • Community Change. Not confirmed. Would speak to the artist organizing work Columbine is part of.

Two things worth watching

Mic and call-and-response, not intellectual marketing. The 8/20 notes name this explicitly. If the panel drifts into organizational messaging, the facilitators should pull it back to the room.

10 to 30 pilot participants will be in that audience — some leading state coalition policy work, some in storytelling cohorts. They are not a passive audience and the session should not treat them as one.

Questions for the panel

Drafted in the design doc. Grouped here by what each one is actually digging at.

Barriers the field built

"What are some traditional barriers to community that we should do away with?"

"Are there misconceptions of basic income that have made it hard to socialize beyond your pilot community?"

Listening for: research norms that keep participants apart. The 8/20 notes name one directly — not wanting participants to speak to one another.

What happens when a pilot ends

"How does your organization stay in touch with people who participated, beyond the pilot?"

"What feels unsettling about the way pilots typically sunset, and what are your hopes for a soft landing? How does the union energise you and your organization?"

This is the union's opening. Most administrators are uneasy about how pilots end and rarely get asked.

What the data misses

"What was the most unexpected outcome of your pilot that has stuck with you and reshaped how you think?"

"Which findings are most persuasive to policymakers — and what are you noticing in the gap between what policymakers want and what participants actually need?"

That gap is the union's whole argument. If a panellist names it themselves, let it sit.

Consulting the union

"Community voice is always there — but how are the important stories actually being shared, and who decides?"

For the facilitators

You do not have to get through all of these. Four answered properly beats nine skimmed. Pick the three you most want on the record and let the room take the rest.

Design Sprint

80 minutes. Room 1 of the breakouts.

An opportunity to design a solution for lifting participant power across the movement — and to create things the union will actually try to implement.

BISC organizers are there to guide and support, not to run it. The people in the room do the work.

Who is running it

Zyera · Columbine · Chemareéa · Faye · Florence

What comes out of the room

Pitches. Individually or in small teams. Each one names a need, an audience, and a thing to build.

The top three get a meeting with the union. Say that at the start — it changes how seriously people take the hour, and it is a promise the union then has to keep.

Before anyone leaves

Photograph every sheet. Paper gets left in hotel conference rooms. A photo takes ten seconds and is the only copy that survives the flight home.

Collect names and contact details for anyone who pitched. You cannot follow up on a good idea with no name attached.

The sprint

The structure from the 8/20 notes, laid out as a worksheet. Roughly 80 minutes.

1

10 minutes

Identify a need

"What is the challenge you would like to approach?"On your own or with two or three others. Write it as a problem, not a solution.
2

15 minutes

Ideas for solutions

Generate, do not evaluate. Quantity first. The judging comes later and someone else's bad idea is often the start of your good one.

3

10 minutes

Who is your audience?

Be specific. "The community" is not an audience. Pilot participants in one state, or county supervisors, or people who have never heard of guaranteed income — those are audiences.

4

10 minutes

What is the messaging?

One sentence that would land with that audience. Not a mission statement.

5

15 minutes

What would you build?

"What is a cool medium that will activate your audience and get folks excited?"A zine, a video, a workshop, a tool, a campaign, a card game. Analogue counts.
6

10 minutes

How could the union help?

The bridge from idea to something real. People, money, reach, credibility, or a room to test it in. Be honest about what would actually be needed.

7

10 minutes

Pitch

Need, audience, blurb. Ninety seconds each. Three sentences is plenty.

Then the room picks. Top three get a meeting with the union.

The one-page worksheet

Print one per person. Six boxes, in this order:

  • The need I want to work on
  • Ideas for solving it
  • Who this is for
  • What I would say to them, in one sentence
  • The thing I would build, and in what medium
  • What the union could do to help

A case worth having ready

The Marie Equity Center came up in the 8/20 notes as a reference — basic income aimed at a specific community, with members doing the community work themselves. Useful if the room stalls on what "a thing we could build" means.

Materials

Print and pack the week before. One owner. Ticks save on this device.

Access

Small-group work assumes people can move, see, hear and write. Ask what people need as they arrive, not after they have struggled. Someone who cannot write on a flip chart can still shape what goes on it — pair them with a writer rather than leaving them out of that part.

Two things to hold

One moment, not a summary. What people remember is a specific detail — a meeting, a decision, something someone said. Not a list of achievements.

Nobody has to share personal experience. This is the story of the work, not of anyone’s circumstances. If you want to tell a piece of your own, that is your choice and nobody else’s expectation.

If someone is in the wrong room

It will happen. Say it warmly and specifically — nobody should feel they turned up somewhere they were not wanted.

"You are in the right building. If you want to get stuck into building something, the sprint is in [room]. If you would rather talk to someone about what the union is, the lounge is in [room]."

Then point, or walk them. Do not describe a route.

Afterward

All three teams together, that evening or the next morning:

  • What landed in each room
  • How many people, and did they end up in the right one
  • Who to follow up with, by name
  • The top three pitches, and who owns each now
  • What we would change

Write it down. It is the only reason the next one is better.

Content consent

We will be collecting photos, quotes, gratitude leaves, wishes and postcards. Every one of those is something a person made, and they should know what happens to it before they make it.

This is the union practicing what its own curriculum teaches. Track 1 tells members to decide what they share before anyone asks. We should not run a room that makes that hard.

Five questions to answer before the day

  • Who owns the content? The person who made it, or the union?
  • How will it be used? Website and app, social media, campaigns, recruitment, education — name them.
  • What rights does the creator keep?
  • How does someone ask for it to be removed later? And who honors that?
  • Can they take part anonymously? First name only, no face, no attribution.

What to have in the room

  • A short slip at each activity: yes to photos, yes to quotes, name or anonymous, and how to reach them
  • Someone able to explain it in one sentence without reading from a card
  • A clear way to say no that does not stop them joining in

Anonymity should be a normal option, not a special request. If someone has to ask for it, most people will not.

Say it plainly

"We would love to share some of this — on our site, or social. Only if you are happy. Tick the box, or tell one of us, and it stays in the room."
